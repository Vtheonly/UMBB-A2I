# 2.2. The Subnetting Cheat Sheet

## 1. Introduction
The key to solving subnetting problems quickly and accurately—without getting bogged down in complex binary calculations—is a simple, three-row cheat sheet. This tool is easy to create and will allow you to solve for all seven subnetting attributes in 60 seconds or less.

If you are taking a certification exam, you cannot bring notes in with you. Therefore, learning to draw this cheat sheet from memory in under a minute is an essential skill.

---

## 2. How to Draw the Cheat Sheet: Step-by-Step

There are only three simple steps to creating the cheat sheet for the fourth octet (the most common for subnetting problems).

#### Step 1: Create the "Group Size" Row
This is the top row of the cheat sheet.
- **Action:** Start with the number `1` in the far-right column. Moving left, **double the value** in each subsequent column until you reach `128`.
- **Result:**

| 128 | 64 | 32 | 16 | 8 | 4 | 2 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

> [!TIP] A good way to remember this is that it's the standard binary positional values for an 8-bit number, which you should already have memorized.

#### Step 2: Create the "Subnet Mask" Row
This is the middle row. Each value in this row is derived from the "Group Size" value directly above it.
- **Action:** For each column, calculate `256 - [Group Size]`.
- **Example Calculation:**
  - `256 - 1 = 255`
  - `256 - 2 = 254`
  - `256 - 4 = 252`
  - ...and so on.
- **Result:**\

| 128 | 64 | 32 | 16 | 8 | 4 | 2 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 128 | 192 | 224 | 240 | 248 | 252 | 254 | 255 |

#### Step 3: Create the "CIDR Notation" Row
This is the bottom row.
- **Action:** Start with `/32` in the far-right column. Moving left, **decrement the value by 1** for each column.
- **Result:**

| 128 | 64 | 32 | 16 | 8 | 4 | 2 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 128 | 192 | 224 | 240 | 248 | 252 | 254 | 255 |
| /25 | /26 | /27 | /28 | /29 | /30 | /31 | /32 |

---

## 3. The Completed Cheat Sheet

Here is the finished product. This simple table is the foundation for the entire rapid subnetting method.

| Group Size | 128 | 64 | 32 | 16 | 8 | 4 | 2 | 1 |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Subnet Mask** | 128 | 192 | 224 | 240 | 248 | 252 | 254 | 255 |
| **CIDR Notation** | /25 | /26 | /27 | /28 | /29 | /30 | /31 | /32 |

---

## 4. Understanding the Role of Each Row

Each row provides a critical piece of information that maps directly to the other rows in the same column.

-   **Group Size (Top Row):** This is the **most important row**. It tells you two things:
    1.  The **total number of IP addresses** in a subnet of that size.
    2.  The **increment value** or "block size". This is the multiple by which network IDs are determined (e.g., for a `/27`, the networks are `0, 32, 64, 96...`).

-   **Subnet Mask (Middle Row):** This value represents the number that will appear in the "interesting" octet of the subnet mask.
    -   For a `/26` network, the subnet mask is `255.255.255.192`. The `192` comes directly from this row.

-   **CIDR Notation (Bottom Row):** This is the shorthand notation that corresponds to the subnet mask and group size in that column.

#### How to Use It (A Quick Preview)

If a problem gives you a `/28` network:
1.  You immediately find the `/28` column.
2.  You know the **Group Size** is `16`. This means there are 16 total IPs, and the network IDs will be multiples of 16 (0, 16, 32, 48...).
3.  You know the subnet mask will end in `.240`. So, it's `255.255.255.240`.
