# 2.6. Subnetting in Higher Octets (CIDR /1 to /24)

## 1. Introduction
While most introductory subnetting problems focus on the fourth octet (CIDR notations `/25` to `/32`), real-world networks often use larger subnets that involve the first, second, and third octets. The good news is that **the process for solving these problems is nearly identical**. The only difference is being mindful of which octet you are working in.

---

## 2. Extending the Cheat Sheet
To handle larger subnets, we simply continue the pattern of the cheat sheet to the left.

- **Third Octet (CIDR /17 to /24):** We add a new row for CIDR notations from `/17` to `/24`. The "Group Size" and "Subnet Mask" rows maintain their pattern.
- **Second Octet (CIDR /9 to /16):** We add another row for `/9` to `/16`.
- **First Octet (CIDR /1 to /8):** We add a final row for `/1` to `/8`.

### The Fully Extended Cheat Sheet

| 128 | 64 | 32 | 16 | 8 | 4 | 2 | 1 |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| 128 | 192 | 224 | 240 | 248 | 252 | 254 | 255 |
| /25 | /26 | /27 | /28 | /29 | /30 | /31 | /32 |

| /9 | /10 | /11 | /12 | /13 | /14 | /15 | /16 |
| /1 | /2 | /3 | /4 | /5 | /6 | /7 | /8 |

> [!NOTE] The top two rows (Group Size and Subnet Mask value) remain the same for every octet. The only thing that changes is the CIDR notation and which octet you apply the logic to.

## 3. Subnetting in the Third Octet (Example)

The key difference is that the "interesting" octet—the one where we perform our increments—is now the third octet.

**Problem:** `10.4.77.188 /19`

1.  **Identify the Octet:** A `/19` CIDR falls in the third octet (between /17 and /24). This is our "interesting" octet.

2.  **Find the Column:** Locate `/19` on the cheat sheet.
    - **Group Size:** `32`
    - **Subnet Mask Value:** `224`
    - The full mask is `255.255.224.0`. Notice the `224` is in the third octet, and everything to the right is `0`.

3.  **List Increments in the "Interesting" Octet:**
    - **Target Value:** `77` (from the third octet of the IP)
    - **Group Size:** `32`
    - **Action:** List multiples of `32` in the third octet until we pass `77`.
      - `10.4.0.0`
      - `10.4.32.0`
      - `10.4.64.0`
      - `10.4.96.0`

    > ** reminder: Octet Rules**
    > - Octets to the left of the "interesting" one are inherited from the original IP (`10.4`).
    > - The "interesting" octet is where increments happen.
    > - Octets to the right of the "interesting" one are always set to `0` when determining Network IDs.

4.  **Box the Range:** Our target IP `10.4.77.188` falls between `10.4.64.0` and `10.4.96.0`.

5.  **Identify Attributes:**
    -   **Network ID:** `10.4.64.0`
    -   **Next Network:** `10.4.96.0`
    -   **Broadcast IP:** The IP right before `10.4.96.0` is `10.4.95.255`.
    -   **First Host IP:** `10.4.64.1`
    -   **Last Host IP:** `10.4.95.254`

6.  **Number of IPs:**
    This is the other major difference. We can't just use the group size.
    - **Formula:** `2 ^ (32 - CIDR)`
    - **Calculation:** `2 ^ (32 - 19) = 2 ^ 13 = 8,192` total addresses.
    - **Usable:** `8192 - 2 = 8,190`.

## 4. Subnetting in the Second Octet (Example)

The logic continues. Now the second octet is the "interesting" one.

**Problem:** `10.51.11.22 /12`

1.  **Identify the Octet:** `/12` is in the second octet.
2.  **Cheat Sheet:**
    - **Group Size:** `16`
    - **Subnet Mask Value:** `240`
    - **Full Mask:** `255.240.0.0`
3.  **List Increments in the Second Octet:**
    - **Target:** `51`
    - **Group Size:** `16`
    - `10.0.0.0`, `10.16.0.0`, `10.32.0.0`, `10.48.0.0`, `10.64.0.0`
4.  **Box the Range:** `10.51.11.22` is between `10.48.0.0` and `10.64.0.0`.
5.  **Identify Attributes:**
    -   **Network ID:** `10.48.0.0`
    -   **Next Network:** `10.64.0.0`
    -   **Broadcast IP:** `10.63.255.255`
    -   **First Host IP:** `10.48.0.1`
    -   **Last Host IP:** `10.63.255.254`
6.  **Number of IPs:**
    - `2 ^ (32 - 12) = 2 ^ 20 = 1,048,576` total addresses.

---

## 5. Subnetting in the First Octet (Example)

**Problem:** `213.51.11.22 /2`

1.  **Identify the Octet:** `/2` is in the first octet.
2.  **Cheat Sheet:**
    - **Group Size:** `64`
    - **Subnet Mask Value:** `192`
    - **Full Mask:** `192.0.0.0`
3.  **List Increments in the First Octet:**
    - **Target:** `213`
    - **Group Size:** `64`
    - `0.0.0.0`, `64.0.0.0`, `128.0.0.0`, `192.0.0.0`, `256.0.0.0` -> This is the end of the internet, so the last block runs to `255.255.255.255`.
4.  **Box the Range:** `213.51.11.22` is between `192.0.0.0` and the end.
5.  **Identify Attributes:**
    -   **Network ID:** `192.0.0.0`
    -   **Broadcast IP:** `255.255.255.255`
    -   **Next Network:** Does not exist. This is the last possible block of this size.
    -   **First Host IP:** `192.0.0.1`
    -   **Last Host IP:** `255.255.255.254`
6.  **Number of IPs:**
    - `2 ^ (32 - 2) = 2 ^ 30 = ~1.07 billion` addresses.

**Key Takeaway:** The method is universal. Identify the "interesting" octet based on the CIDR value, then apply the same incrementing logic within that octet. Remember to set octets to the right to `0` for Network IDs and `255` for Broadcast IPs as needed.
